\title{The randomly oriented Manhattan lattice in 2D is transient}
\author{Ahmed Bou-Rabee}
\author{Yuval Peres}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Independently orient each horizontal and vertical line of $\Z^2$ by a fair
coin.  A walker chooses one of the two lines through its current position
with equal probability and takes one step in the direction of that line.
Redner \citeyearpar{Red89} introduced this walk as a model of transport in
an isotropic random velocity field and predicted that its root-mean-square
displacement grows like $n^{2/3}$.  We prove that the walk is transient almost
surely.  The proof is a short variational argument.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

The \defn{randomly oriented Manhattan lattice} is the square lattice with
every horizontal and vertical line given a direction by an independent fair
coin.  We study the random walk that at each step chooses one of the two
lines through its position with equal probability and moves one unit in that
line's direction (Figure~\ref{fig:model}).

Once a line is oriented, every later step along it has the same direction.
Figure~\ref{fig:path} compares the walk with simple random walk.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{fig_paths.pdf}
\caption{The randomly oriented Manhattan walk on the left and simple random
walk on the right, after $400$ steps in the top row and $4000$ steps in the
bottom row, colored by time from blue to red.  The two panels of each row are
drawn to the same scale.}
\label{fig:path}
\end{figure}

\citet*[(3) and p.~290]{Red89} introduced this walk and predicted a
non-Gaussian displacement profile on the scale $n^{2/3}$.
The corresponding heat-kernel prediction would imply transience.
We prove that the walk is transient.

\subsection{Main results}\label{ssec:main-results}

The expected total number of visits to the starting point, averaged over
the environment, is finite.  This implies transience for almost every
environment.  Write $e_1,e_2$ for the standard basis of $\Z^2$, and record the
line orientations by
$\omega\colon\Z^2\times\{1,2\}\to\{-1,1\}$: the line through $z$ parallel
to $e_i$ points along $\omega(z,i)e_i$.  Thus $\omega(z,i)$ does not depend
on $z_i$.  We call $\omega$ the \defn{environment} and write $\P$ for its law.

From $z$ the walker steps to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rule}
  z+\omega(z,1)e_1\qquad\text{or}\qquad z+\omega(z,2)e_2,
  \qquad\text{with probability $1/2$ each.}
\end{equation}
Since $\omega(z,i)=\omega(z-e_i,i)$, the drift has vanishing discrete
divergence.  Every site has two incoming and two outgoing directed edges,
so counting measure is invariant.

Write $P^\omega_z$ for the quenched law of the walk started at $z$ in a fixed
environment $\omega$, and write $p^\omega_n(z,z')$ for the probability under
$P^\omega_z$ that the walk is at $z'$ after $n$ steps.
Averaging over $\omega$ gives the annealed transition probability
$\overline p_n(z,z')=\E p^\omega_n(z,z')$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
For $\P$-almost every environment $\omega$,
\[
  \sum_{n\geq0}p^\omega_n(x,x)<\infty\qquad\text{for every }x\in\Z^2\, .
\]
\end{theorem}

It is convenient to work in continuous time, letting the walker jump along
each of its two outgoing edges at rate $1$.  The total jump rate is $2$,
and the jump chain is the walk in \eqref{eq:rule}.  An annealed Green-function
bound will imply Theorem~\ref{thm:main}.  Write
$p^\omega_t(z,z')$ for its transition probabilities and set
$\overline p_t(0,0)=\E p^\omega_t(0,0)$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:annealed}
There is a constant $C<\infty$ with
\[
  \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\overline p_t(0,0)\dd t\leq C
  \qquad\text{for all }\lambda\in(0,1]\, ,
\]
and hence $\int_0^\infty\overline p_t(0,0)\dd t<\infty$.
\end{theorem}

Transience does not hold for every environment.  Take the alternating
orientations,
$\omega(z,1)=(-1)^{z_2}$ and $\omega(z,2)=(-1)^{z_1}$.  For the walk started at $0$, halving and rounding down both coordinates
at successive even times gives simple random walk on $\Z^2$.
Thus the alternating walk is recurrent.
\citet*[p.~2]{BH23} report this observation of Guillotin-Plantard.

\subsection{Proof overview}\label{ssec:proof-overview}

We prove transience by bounding the annealed Green function.  For a
reversible walk, a unit flow from a vertex to infinity with finite energy
suffices to prove transience
\citep*[Theorem~2.11]{LP16}.  Our variational bound has the same advantage:
we need only exhibit one test function.  Here that function will depend on
the independent line orientations.

The proof proceeds in three steps.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\arabic*)}]
\item Fourier transformation reduces the Green-function bound to a
variational problem at each spatial frequency.  Ignoring the orientations
gives an upper bound that diverges as the Laplace parameter tends to zero.
\item We construct the test function from products of one and three line
signs.  The degree-three part improves the bound left by degree one.
One real even function determines both parts.
\item Optimizing this function gives a logarithmic improvement with exponent
$3/2$.  This makes the frequency integral finite and proves transience.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 1.}  Fix $\lambda\in(0,1]$.  Translation invariance of the
environment law allows us to Fourier transform in the position of the walk.
At frequency $p$, the transformed generator is $S_p+A_p$, with $S_p$
self-adjoint and $A_p$ skew-adjoint.  Put $H=\lambda I-S_p$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:generator} gives
\[
  \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\overline p_t(0,0)\dd t
  =\int_{\T^2}r_\lambda(p)\dd m(p)\, ,
\]
where $r_\lambda(p)=\operatorname{Re}\ip1{(H-A_p)^{-1}1}$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:variational-bound} gives, for every $g\in L^2(\P)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:overview-bound}
  r_\lambda(p)\leq
  \ip g{Hg}+\ip{1-A_pg}{H^{-1}(1-A_pg)}\, .
\end{equation}
The two terms measure the energy of $g$ and the residual $1-A_pg$.
We want $A_pg$ to approximate the constant $1$ without making the energy
large.  Taking $g=0$ gives
$(\lambda+2-\cos p_1-\cos p_2)^{-1}$, whose integral diverges logarithmically
as $\lambda\downarrow0$.  The task is to improve this bound near $p=0$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 2.}  Products of distinct line signs form the
Fourier--Walsh basis; the degree of a product is its number of signs.
The operator $H$ preserves degree, while $A_p$ adds or removes one sign.
We therefore start with a degree-one function $f$ to reduce the constant
residual.  After interchanging the axes when necessary, we take $f$ to use
only row signs.  Applying $A_p$ also creates a degree-two residual, with
two components: one containing two row signs, the other a row sign and a
column sign.

We reduce the second component by adding a degree-three function $k$ built
from two row signs and one column sign.  Adding $k$ costs energy and
creates a degree-four residual.  We choose $k$ by minimizing a quadratic
upper bound for its energy and the squared weighted norms of the
degree-four residual and the component with one row and one column.
The Fourier coefficient of $f$ is $-i\varphi(r)$, where $r$ is the shifted
row frequency and $\varphi$ is real and even.  The minimization determines
$k$ from $\varphi$, so one function determines the entire test function
$g=f+k$.

The row signs in $k$ must be distinct: a repeated sign squares to $1$,
leaving a product of degree one.  We enforce distinctness by deleting
coefficients with coincident row indices.  For our choice of coefficient,
the resulting change in the component with one row and one column is a
sum of integrals of functions odd in a row frequency.  Each integral vanishes.
The degree-three coefficient
is also odd in the column frequency, so $k$ leaves the two-row residual
unchanged.  Lemma~\ref{lem:parity} proves these cancellations and the
quadratic bound used to choose $k$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Step 3.}  We estimate and optimize the contribution of
$\varphi$.  Put $a=\max\{\abs{p_1},\abs{p_2}\}$ and
$\delta=\sqrt\lambda+a$.  For $\delta<1/256$, we take $\varphi$ supported on
$\sqrt\delta\leq\abs r\leq1/4$.
Integrating over the extra row frequency in the correction
produces a logarithm.  The subsequent integration in the column frequency
leaves the weight
$q(r)=\abs r/\sqrt{\log(1/\abs r)}$ of \eqref{eq:q}.
Lemma~\ref{lem:effective-energy} bounds the energy and all nonconstant
residual terms by $16\int q\varphi^2\dd m$.  For fixed $\lambda,p$, the
constant residual depends only on $\int\varphi\dd m$.  We can therefore
first fix this integral and minimize $\int q\varphi^2\dd m$.
Equality in Cauchy--Schwarz prescribes $\varphi$ proportional to $q^{-1}$
on $\sqrt\delta\leq\abs r\leq1/4$.

The reciprocal weight satisfies
\[
  \int_{\sqrt\delta\leq\abs r\leq1/4}\frac{\dd m(r)}{q(r)}
  \asymp[\log(1/\delta)]^{3/2}\, ,
\]
by the substitution $u=\log(1/\abs r)$.
Section~\ref{sec:fixed-frequency} optimizes the remaining proportionality
constant and uses $g=0$ when $\delta\geq1/256$ to give
Proposition~\ref{prop:frequency}.  In particular, for $\sqrt\lambda<a<1/4$,
\[
  r_\lambda(p)\leq\frac{C}{a^2[1+\log(1/a)]^{3/2}}\, .
\]
Since $\{\max_i\abs{p_i}\leq a\}$ has area $4a^2$, integrating this bound
over $p$ reduces to
\[
  \int_0^{1/4}\frac{\dd a}{a[1+\log(1/a)]^{3/2}}<\infty\, .
\]
The region $a\leq\sqrt\lambda$ is controlled by
$r_\lambda(p)\leq1/\lambda$, since its area is $4\lambda$.
The region $a\geq1/4$ is bounded using $g=0$.
The annealed Green function is therefore finite
(Theorem~\ref{thm:annealed}).  Tonelli's theorem and the change of clock
then give transience.

\subsection{Related work}\label{ssec:related-work}

We compare the known displacement bounds, the theory of divergence-free
drifts, and the variational methods used in the proof.

\subsubsection{Random walks on oriented lattices}\label{sssec:oriented}

Redner developed the model with J.~Koplik, F.~Leyvraz, and A.~Provata
\citep*[p.~287]{Red89}.

Redner obtained the exponent $\nu=2/3$ by the following heuristic
self-consistency argument \citep*[p.~290]{Red89}.  Suppose that the
horizontal displacement by time $t$ has scale $R=R(t)$.  Of the $R$
vertical lines the walk encounters, about half point up and half down,
but there is typically a surplus of order $\sqrt{R}$ in one of these
directions.  Since each of these lines is visited about $t/R$ times on
average, this yields a typical vertical displacement of order
$t/\sqrt{R}$.  Isotropy of the model then yields $t/\sqrt{R}\approx R$,
so $R\approx t^{2/3}$; see also
\citet*[p.~152, item~\textup{(v)} and (1.73a)--(1.73b)]{BG90}.
The same heuristic predicts displacement exponent $2/(d+1)$
in dimensions $2\leq d\leq3$.

More specifically, \citet*[(3) and p.~290]{Red89} reports a one-coordinate
displacement profile proportional to $t^{-2/3}\exp(-c|x|^3/t^2)$.
A two-dimensional local limit on this scale, with finite positive density at
the origin, would give return probabilities of order $t^{-4/3}$.
Simulations support this decay rate
\citep*[Section~3.2]{MNOV20}.
\citet*[Section~3.5]{Pen20} records the displacement conjecture.
\citet*[Appendix~A, Conjecture~1 and reference~\textup{[10]}]{Bos19}
attributes an explicit transience conjecture to Guillotin-Plantard's
2014 New Zealand Probability Workshop talk.
Transience remained open in
\citet*[p.~1]{CHT19} and \citet*[p.~1]{BH23}.

\citet*[Theorem~1.1]{LTV18} proved two-sided bounds on the Laplace
transform of the annealed mean-square displacement in dimensions two and
three.  \citet*[Theorem~1.1]{Ngu26} recently presented a deep analysis of
the three-dimensional case.  He proved that, for every $\varepsilon>0$
and all sufficiently small $\lambda$, this Laplace transform is of order
$\lambda^{-2}\sqrt{\abs{\log\lambda}}$, up to multiplicative factors
$(\log\abs{\log\lambda})^{\pm(2+\varepsilon)}$.
This establishes the predicted leading logarithmic power.

When only horizontal lines are oriented, the vertical coordinate evolves
autonomously, as in the layered-flow model of \citet*[p.~901]{MdM80}.
With independent fair horizontal orientations and uniform choice among
the three outgoing edges, the walk is almost surely transient
\citep*[Theorem~1.8]{CP03} and has zero speed
\citep*[Theorem~2.11]{CP04}.  For this walk,
\citet*[Theorem~2]{GLN08F} proved an annealed joint functional limit theorem
with horizontal scaling $n^{3/4}$ and vertical scaling $n^{1/2}$; the
limiting components are dependent.  The annealed return probability has
order $n^{-5/4}$ along even times
\citep*[Theorem~20 and Remark~21]{CGPS11}.

\citet*[Section~2.1, Theorem~1 and (2.3)]{GLN08T} extended transience to
orientations sampled independently conditional on the initial point of a
measure-preserving dynamical system, under their integrability condition
(2.3).  Averaging over the initial point also covers certain correlated
orientation laws.  These arguments use the autonomous vertical walk.
In the Manhattan model, each coordinate depends on the orientations
encountered by the other, so the same reduction is unavailable.

For prescribed horizontal orientations, the walk belongs to the stratified
class of \citet*[p.~752]{Bre17}.  His recurrence criterion uses directional
flux functions \citep*[Definition~2.3 and Theorem~2.4]{Bre17}, whose
variations describe directional macrodispersion \citep*[p.~753]{Bre17}.
The pattern pointing east at nonnegative heights and west below is
transient \citep*[Example~1.4 and Theorem~1.7]{CP03}.
For this pattern, \citet*[Theorem~1.2 and Corollary~2.6]{BHP22} obtained
return probability of order $n^{-3/2}$ just after the $2n$th vertical step.
The second lattice of \citet*[Section~1.1]{BHP22}, obtained by changing
departures from the horizontal axis, is recurrent
\citep*[Proposition~7.8]{Bre17}, with first-return-time tail at the origin
$(\log k)^{-1+o(1)}$ \citep*[Theorem~1.4]{BHP22}.

For the classical Manhattan lattice with alternating deterministic
orientations, \citet*[Theorem~1]{BH23} computed exactly the mean-square
displacement.  That walk is diffusive and recurrent, so randomizing the
orientations turns a recurrent walk into a transient one.

Other dynamics on Manhattan lattices behave differently.
For the deterministic update $z\mapsto z+\omega(z,1)e_1+\omega(z,2)e_2$,
\citet*[Theorem~1]{CHT19} proved eventual two-periodicity for
$\P$-almost every environment.
\citet*[pp.~10251--10254]{BOC03} study quantum and classical network models
on the alternating deterministic lattice.

\subsubsection{Divergence-free drifts}\label{sssec:divfree}

The Manhattan drift is divergence-free and has long-range correlations
\citep*[p.~2506]{BGKPR90}.  The $\Hs_{-1}$ condition requires the spectral
measure of each drift component to be
integrable against the reciprocal of the symbol of the negative discrete
Laplacian.  Equivalently, the drift must admit a stationary
square-integrable stream tensor
\citep*[(10) and Proposition~1\textup{(ii)}]{Tot18}.

For stationary ergodic environments with bounded jump rates,
\citet*[Theorem~1]{KT17} proved convergence of the diffusively rescaled
finite-dimensional distributions to Brownian motion, in probability with
respect to the environment.  They assume double stochasticity, zero
annealed drift, uniform ellipticity of the symmetric rates, and the
$\Hs_{-1}$ condition.  With the additional $L^{2+\varepsilon}$ integrability of the
stream tensor, \citet*[Theorem~1\textup{(ii)}]{Tot18Q} proved the quenched
central limit theorem.  The martingale approximation in
\citet*[Theorem~RSC2]{KT17} extends the relaxed sector condition of
\citet*[Theorem~1]{HTV12}.
For the Manhattan model, the $\Hs_{-1}$ condition
fails with a power-law divergence in dimension two, fails logarithmically
in dimension three, and holds in dimensions at least four
\citep*[Section~1.4]{Tot18}.

For walks with a bounded cycle representation,
\citet*[Theorem~1.1 and assumptions~\textup{(a)--(c)}, p.~575]{DK08} prove
a quenched invariance principle under coordinatewise ergodicity, upper and
lower bounds on the invariant density, and uniform strong irreducibility.

The continuum analogue is Brownian motion in an incompressible random
drift.  \citet*[Theorem~1 and (1.2)--(1.5)]{Oel88} proved homogenization
with a square-integrable stream matrix and drift.
\citet*[Theorem~1\textup{(ii)}, pp.~1874--1877]{FK97} proved a quenched
invariance principle with a stationary stream matrix having a finite $p$th
moment for some $p>d$ and a square-integrable drift.  Both results require ergodicity and impose regularity and growth
assumptions.

The line correlations also appear in the discrete stream function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stream}
  \psi^\omega(z+e_2)-\psi^\omega(z)=\omega(z,1),
  \qquad
  \psi^\omega(z+e_1)-\psi^\omega(z)=-\omega(z,2),
  \qquad \psi^\omega(0)=0\, .
\end{equation}
Along each coordinate, $\psi^\omega$ is a sum of independent signs.
Replacing these sums by their Brownian scaling limits gives the formal
analogue
\[
  \psi(x,y)=W_1(y)-W_2(x),
  \qquad f=(\partial_y\psi,-\partial_x\psi)\, .
\]
Here $W_1,W_2$ are independent two-sided Brownian motions, and the
derivatives in $f$ are distributional.  Its first component depends only
on $y$, and its second only on $x$.

In dimension $d$, the covariance of a Manhattan drift component is
supported on its coordinate axis.  The spatial average of this covariance
over a box of side $R$ centered at the origin has order $R^{1-d}$.
Matching this decay with an isotropic covariance of order
$\abs{x-y}^{-2(1-\gamma)}$, as in \citet*[(1.2)]{ABK26}, gives the heuristic
correspondence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma-dimension}
  \gamma=\frac{3-d}{2}\, .
\end{equation}
The renormalization-group prediction in \citet*[(1.3)]{ABK26} then gives
annealed mean-square displacement of order $t^{4/3}$ in dimension two, $t(\log t)^{1/2}$ in
dimension three, and $t$ in higher dimensions.

For the curl of the mollified two-dimensional Gaussian free field,
\citet*[Theorem~1 and Remark~2]{TV12} proved superdiffusivity and predicted
annealed mean-square displacement of order $t(\log t)^{1/2}$.
\citet*[Theorem~1.1]{CMOW25} proved this order.
\citet*[Theorem~A and Section~1.2]{ABK24} proved a
quenched invariance principle at this scaling for their multiscale drifts,
including the mollified Gaussian free field.
For multiscale stream matrices with sufficiently small Hurst exponent
$\gamma>0$, \citet*[Theorem~A and assumptions~\textup{(J1)--(J4)}]{ABK26}
proved moment bounds for the quenched mean-square displacement at scale
$t^{2/(2-\gamma)}$.  These results do not cover the line correlations of
the Manhattan model, so \eqref{eq:gamma-dimension} remains a heuristic
comparison.

\subsubsection{The variational formula}\label{sssec:variational}

Our proof uses a resolvent formula for nonreversible Markov processes.
Earlier variational principles for effective diffusivity appear in
\citet*[Section~V.1, (5.20)]{AM91},
\citet*[Sections~4.1--4.2.1, (4.22) and (4.43)--(4.44)]{FP96}, and
\citet*[Proposition~5.1, (5.1)--(5.2)]{LOY98}.
For a generator $L=S+A$, with $S$ self-adjoint and $A$ skew-adjoint,
\citet*[Theorem~4.1]{KLO12} express
$\operatorname{Re}\ip V{(\lambda I-L)^{-1}V}$ as the infimum of a
quadratic functional.  The domain condition holds here because
$H$, $H^{-1}$, and $A_p$ are bounded.  Related formulas appear in
\citet*[(2.17)]{LQSY04} and \citet*[(39)]{Tot18}.
For capacities of nonreversible Markov chains,
\citet*[Theorem~2.4 and Section~2.3]{GL14} prove a min--max principle when
one boundary set has finite complement.

Restricting a resolvent supremum to degree one gives superdiffusive lower
bounds for the stationary one-dimensional self-repelling Brownian polymer
\citep*[(92) and (98)--(100)]{TTV12}.
For the Manhattan walk, \citet*[(1.7), (2.1), and Lemma~2.1]{LTV18}
use a degree-one test function in a resolvent matrix element of the drift
to bound the Laplace transform of the annealed mean-square displacement
from below.  Our proof of transience needs an upper bound for $r_\lambda(p)$ in
\eqref{eq:frequency-resolvent}, whose source is the constant function $1$.
The degree-three correction improves this upper bound.  Parity allows us to exclude repeated line
indices exactly; \citet*[Sections~3--4]{LQSY04} handle the corresponding
constraint for the exclusion process by comparison estimates.
\citet*[Theorems~1.1--1.2 and (1.5)]{Yau04} obtained logarithmic exponent
$2/3$ for the bulk diffusion coefficient of a two-dimensional asymmetric
exclusion process at density $1/2$, in the Laplace-transform sense and up
to subleading factors.  Our exponent $3/2$ concerns the return Green function rather than
the diffusion coefficient.

The same quadratic functional occurs in quantitative homogenization.
For the bilinear form $\operatorname{Re}\ip v{(H+A_p)u}$, twice the
functional of \citet*[Exercise~10.1, immediately before (10.13)]{AKM19}
at $(g,1)$ is the right side of \eqref{eq:overview-bound}.
For symmetric coefficients, the variational quantities are the Dirichlet
energy and its convex dual \citep*[(2.1)--(2.2)]{AS16}
\citep*[(2.6)--(2.9)]{AKM19}.
\citet*[(2.4)--(2.5)]{ABK24} and \citet*[(2.32)--(2.37)]{ABK26} use
variational quantities on boxes to compare diffusivity at successive
scales.  In our proof, optimizing one function of a row frequency gives
the required return bound.

\section{The variational bound}\label{sec:variational}

We express the annealed Green function as an integral of resolvent matrix
elements and bound each by a quadratic functional.

\subsection{Fourier transform}\label{ssec:fourier}

We use translation invariance to separate the spatial frequencies.
For a fixed environment and $h\colon\Z^2\to\R$, the generator is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:discrete-drift}
\begin{split}
  (\mathcal G_\omega h)(z)
  &=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^2
    \bigl[h(z+e_i)+h(z-e_i)-2h(z)\bigr]\\
  &\quad+\frac12\sum_{i=1}^2\omega(z,i)
    \bigl[h(z+e_i)-h(z-e_i)\bigr]\, .
\end{split}
\end{equation}
On $\ell^2(\Z^2)$, the first sum is the self-adjoint simple-random-walk
generator.  The second sum is skew-adjoint because $\omega(z,i)$ is
constant along $e_i$.

For $x\in\Z^2$, define the environment shift and its action on functions by
\[
  (T_xF)(\omega)=F(\tau_x\omega),\qquad
  (\tau_x\omega)(z,i)=\omega(z+x,i)\, .
\]
The operator $T_x$ is unitary on $L^2(\P)$, and $T_{\pm e_i}$ commutes
with multiplication by $\omega(0,i)$ because that sign is constant along
the line parallel to $e_i$.

We keep track of both the walker's position and the environment seen
from that position.  The generator of this pair on
$L^2(\P;\ell^2(\Z^2))$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:joint-generator}
  (\mathcal GF)(\omega,z)=\sum_{i=1}^2
  \bigl[F(\tau_{\omega(0,i)e_i}\omega,z+\omega(0,i)e_i)-F(\omega,z)\bigr]\, .
\end{equation}
For returns to the origin, set
$\delta_0(\omega,z)=\1_{\{z=0\}}$.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:return-correlation}
  \overline p_t(0,0)=\ip{\delta_0}{e^{t\mathcal G}\delta_0}\, .
\end{equation}
Under the Fourier transform
$\widehat F(\omega,p)=\sum_z e^{-ip\cdot z}F(\omega,z)$, the function
$F(\tau_x\omega,z+x)$ becomes $e^{ip\cdot x}T_x\widehat F$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:generator}
After Fourier transformation in the position variable, $\mathcal G$ acts
at each frequency $p\in\T^2$ as $G_p=S_p+A_p$, where
\begin{align}
  S_p&=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^2
  \bigl(e^{ip_i}T_{e_i}+e^{-ip_i}T_{-e_i}-2I\bigr),\label{eq:Sp}\\
  A_p&=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^2\omega(0,i)
  \bigl(e^{ip_i}T_{e_i}-e^{-ip_i}T_{-e_i}\bigr)\, .\label{eq:Ap}
\end{align}
Uniformly in $p$, the operator $S_p$ is self-adjoint and nonpositive,
$A_p^*=-A_p$, $\norm{S_p}\leq4$, and $\norm{A_p}\leq2$.
Put $d(s)=1-\cos s$ and $\theta(p)=d(p_1)+d(p_2)$.
For every $\lambda>0$,
\begin{align}
  \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\overline p_t(0,0)\dd t
  &=\int_{\T^2}r_\lambda(p)\dd m(p),\label{eq:green}\\
  r_\lambda(p)&\coloneqq
  \operatorname{Re}\ip1{(\lambda I-G_p)^{-1}1}\, .\label{eq:frequency-resolvent}
\end{align}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Splitting \eqref{eq:joint-generator} according to the two values of each
sign gives \eqref{eq:Sp} and \eqref{eq:Ap}.  Unitarity of the shifts and
their commutation with the corresponding signs give the operator
assertions.  The Fourier transform of $\delta_0$ is $1$, so Plancherel's
theorem and integration of \eqref{eq:return-correlation} against
$e^{-\lambda t}\dd t$ give \eqref{eq:green}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The fixed-frequency bound}\label{ssec:varbound}

We bound the resolvent using a test function and its residual.
Fix $\lambda>0$ and $p\in\T^2$, and put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:H}
  H\coloneqq\lambda I-S_p,\qquad
  \lambda I\leq H\leq(\lambda+4)I,\qquad
  H1=(\lambda+\theta(p))1\, .
\end{equation}
Thus $H^{-1}$ and $H^{-1/2}$ are bounded, and we set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hnorms}
  \Hplus g^2\coloneqq\ip g{Hg},\qquad
  \Hminus u^2\coloneqq\ip u{H^{-1}u}\, .
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:variational-bound}
For every $\lambda>0$, $p\in\T^2$, and $g\in L^2(\P)$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:var}
  0\leq r_\lambda(p)\leq\Hplus g^2+\Hminus{1-A_pg}^2\, .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $z=(H-A_p)^{-1}1$, $d=z+g$, and $q=1-A_pg$.  Then
$q=Hz-A_pd$, and skew-adjointness gives
\[
  r_\lambda(p)=\operatorname{Re}\ip1z=\Hplus z^2,
  \qquad
  \operatorname{Re}\ip qd=\operatorname{Re}\ip{Hz}d\, .
\]
Expanding $z=d-g$ gives
\[
  \Hplus z^2=\Hplus g^2
  +\left(2\operatorname{Re}\ip qd-\Hplus d^2\right)\, .
\]
Weighted Cauchy--Schwarz bounds the bracket by $\Hminus q^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Taking $g=0$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zero-function}
  r_\lambda(p)\leq\Hplus0^2+\Hminus1^2
  =\frac1{\lambda+\theta(p)}\, .
\end{equation}
This bound suffices when $p$ stays away from $0$.
\end{remark}

\section{Fourier--Walsh coordinates}\label{sec:coins}

We evaluate the variational bound by expanding the test function in
products of line signs.  Fix $\lambda>0$ and $p\in\T^2$, and write
$A=A_p$.

For a finite set of line signs, products of distinct signs, including
the empty product $1$, form an orthonormal basis for functions of those signs
\citep*[Theorem~1.5]{ODo14}.  Taking conditional expectations onto
increasing finite sets that exhaust the line signs and applying $L^2$
martingale convergence gives the Fourier--Walsh basis of $L^2(\P)$.

\subsection{Degree and frequency}\label{ssec:degree}

We represent each degree by symmetric coefficients indexed by lines.
Index each line sign by $(x,j)$, where $j\in\{1,2\}$ and
$x\in\{z\in\Z^2:z_j=0\}$.  A sign of type $1$ is a row sign,
and a sign of type $2$ is a column sign.  For $n\geq0$, let $\Hs_n$ be the closed span of
products of $n$ distinct line signs.  Every $F\in\Hs_n$ has a coefficient $f$, symmetric under permutations of
the $n$ line indices $(x_a,j_a)$, such that
\[
  F(\omega)=\sum_{\mathbf x,\mathbf j}f_{\mathbf j}(\mathbf x)
  \prod_{a=1}^n\omega(x_a,j_a)\, .
\]
The sum converges in $L^2(\P)$ and runs over ordered line indices;
$f$ vanishes when two line indices coincide.
If $F\in\Hs_n$ and
$G\in\Hs_m$ have coefficients $f$ and $g$, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:iso}
  \ip FG=\1_{\{m=n\}}n!\ip fg_{\ell^2}\, .
\end{equation}
The factor $n!$ counts the orderings of the same $n$ signs.

Multiplication by $\omega(0,i)$ adds the corresponding line sign when it is
absent and removes it when it is present.  Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:DA}
  A=D-D^*,\qquad D\colon\Hs_n\longrightarrow\Hs_{n+1}\, .
\end{equation}
Here $D$ raises the degree by one and $-D^*$ lowers it by one, as in the
decomposition in \citet*[(2.6)--(2.8)]{LQSY04} for the exclusion process
at density $1/2$.
To exclude repeated signs after raising, let $\Pi_n$ denote the
orthogonal projection onto coefficients supported on distinct line indices.
Write $H_n$ and $D_n$ for the restrictions of $H$ and $D$ to $\Hs_n$.

For the line coefficients we use the Fourier convention
\begin{equation}\label{eq:line-fourier}
  \widehat f_{\mathbf j}(\boldsymbol\xi)
  =\sum_{\mathbf x}e^{i\sum_a x_a\cdot\xi_a}f_{\mathbf j}(\mathbf x)\, ,
\end{equation}
with convergence in $L^2$.  A line frequency $\xi_a$ has zero $j_a$th
coordinate.  Simultaneous shifts now act by scalar multiplication.
We drop the hats and also use $\Pi_n$ for the projection after Fourier
transformation.
For a degree-$n$ coefficient, the total frequency is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:P}
  P=p+\xi_1+\cdots+\xi_n\, .
\end{equation}
Equations~\eqref{eq:Sp} and \eqref{eq:H} give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Hsym}
  \text{$H_n$ is multiplication by }\lambda+\theta(P)\, .
\end{equation}
We will use
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sine}
  \log_+x=\max\{0,\log x\},
  \qquad \frac{2s^2}{\pi^2}\leq d(s)\leq\frac{s^2}{2}
  \quad (\abs s\leq\pi)\, .
\end{equation}
We identify $H_0$ with the scalar $\lambda+\theta(p)$ by which it acts
on constants.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:raise}
Let $\widetilde D_n$ be the raising operator computed without imposing
distinct line indices.  Then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:raise}
  (\widetilde D_nf)_{j_1\dots j_{n+1}}
  =\frac i{n+1}\sum_{a=1}^{n+1}\sin(P_{j_a})
  f_{j_1\dots\widehat{j_a}\dots j_{n+1}}\, ,
\end{equation}
where $P$ is the total frequency after raising and the hat omits the
$a$-th line index.  Moreover,
$D_n=\Pi_{n+1}\widetilde D_n\Pi_n$, and $\Pi_n$ commutes with every bounded
multiplier that is a function of $P$.
\end{lemma}

The commutation statement lets us remove a projection in upper bounds.
If $\Pi$ is an orthogonal projection commuting with a bounded positive
multiplier $N$ with bounded inverse, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:contract}
  \ip{\Pi u}{N^{-1}\Pi u}\leq\ip u{N^{-1}u},\qquad
  \ip{\Pi u}{N\Pi u}\leq\ip u{Nu}\, .
\end{equation}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:raise}]
By \eqref{eq:Ap}, raising first shifts and then multiplies by
$\omega(0,i)$.  On a coefficient of total frequency $P'$, the shift
contributes
$\tfrac12(e^{iP'_i}-e^{-iP'_i})=i\sin(P'_i)$.  Multiplication appends a line
index of type $i$ at the site $0$, whose Fourier factor is $1$.
Symmetrization averages over the $n+1$ positions of the new index.  If it
occupies position $a$, then $P'=P-\xi_a$ and $(\xi_a)_{j_a}=0$, so
$P'_{j_a}=P_{j_a}$.  This gives \eqref{eq:raise}.  The new index must be
distinct from the existing indices, which gives the projection $\Pi_{n+1}$.

Simultaneous translations preserve distinctness, so $\Pi_n$ commutes
with their multipliers.  Uniform approximation by trigonometric polynomials
gives commutation with continuous functions of $P$ in operator norm;
spectral calculus extends this to bounded Borel functions of $P$.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Degrees one and three}\label{ssec:degrees-one-three}\label{ssec:low-coordinates}

We now take $g=f+k$ with $f\in\Hs_1$ and $k\in\Hs_3$.
Apart from the constant residual, the functional in \eqref{eq:var} is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:E}
  E_p(f,k)=\ip f{H_1f}+\ip k{H_3k}
  +\Hminus{D_1f-D_2^*k}^2+\Hminus{D_3k}^2\, .
\end{equation}
We choose $f$ to use only row signs and $k$ to use two row signs and
one column sign in each term.  To center the denominator for one row and
one column at zero, we shift the line frequencies.  Write a row frequency
as $(0,s)$ and a column frequency as $(u,0)$, and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:shift}
  r=p_2+s,\qquad r'=p_2+s',\qquad \beta=p_1+u,
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad \alpha=r+r'-p_2\, .
\end{equation}
The variables $r,r'$ correspond to the two row signs and $\beta$ to the
column sign; $\alpha$ is the second coordinate of $P$ for terms with two
row signs.  The total frequencies are
\[
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
\begin{array}{llll}
  \text{degree $0$:} & P=(p_1,p_2), &\quad
  \text{two rows:} & P=(p_1,\alpha),\\
  \text{one row:} & P=(p_1,r), &\quad
  \text{one row and one column:} & P=(\beta,r),\\
  & &\quad \text{two rows and a column:} & P=(\beta,\alpha)\, .
\end{array}
\]
The shifts preserve Haar measure.  We use these variables throughout the
coefficient calculations.


\subsection{Low-degree operators}\label{ssec:low-operators}

We compute the raising operator on a row coefficient and the lowering
operator on a coefficient with two rows and one column.

Subscripts record the signs: $11$ means two rows, $12$ one row and one
column, and $112$ two rows and one column.  By \eqref{eq:iso}, multiplying
these coefficients by $\sqrt2$, $2$, and $\sqrt{18}$, respectively, makes
the correspondence with Walsh expansions an isometry.  From now on,
we identify each Walsh component with its normalized Fourier coefficient
function.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:formulas}
Let $k(r,r',\beta)$ be a coefficient function for two rows and one column,
symmetric in $r,r'$.  In the variables \eqref{eq:shift} and the
normalization above,
\begin{align}
  \bigl(\widetilde D_2^*k\bigr)_{11}(r,r')
  &=-i\sin(\alpha)\int_\T k(r,r',\beta)\dd m(\beta),\label{eq:D2a}\\
  \bigl(\widetilde D_2^*k\bigr)_{12}(r,\beta)
  &=-i\sqrt2\sin(\beta)\int_\T k(r,r',\beta)\dd m(r')\, .\label{eq:D2b}
\end{align}
Lowering integrates out the removed sign's frequency: the column in the
first formula, and either row in the second.
For $f$ of degree one carrying only a row sign,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:D1}
  (D_0^*f)(p)=-i\sin (p_1)\int_\T f\dd m,\quad
  (\widetilde D_1f)_{11}=\tfrac i{\sqrt2}\sin (p_1)[f(r)+f(r')],\quad
  (\widetilde D_1f)_{12}=i\sin (r)f(r)\, .
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:formulas}]
For a coefficient with subscript $112$, equation~\eqref{eq:raise} gives, before
normalization,
\[
  \frac i3\bigl[\sin\beta f_{12}(r',\beta)
  +\sin\beta f_{12}(r,\beta)+\sin\alpha f_{11}(r,r')\bigr]\, .
\]
Multiplying the coefficients with subscripts $11$, $12$, and $112$ by
$\sqrt2$, $2$, and $\sqrt{18}=3\sqrt2$, then taking adjoints, gives
\eqref{eq:D2a}--\eqref{eq:D2b}; symmetry in $(r,r')$ gives the factor
$\sqrt2$ in \eqref{eq:D2b}.

For a degree-one row coefficient, the total frequency is $(p_1,r)$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:raise} with $n=0$ gives
$(\widetilde D_0u)_1=i\sin(p_1)u$, whose adjoint is the first formula in
\eqref{eq:D1}.  Equation~\eqref{eq:raise} with $n=1$, followed by the
degree-two normalization above, gives the other two formulas.
\end{proof}

\section{The test function}\label{sec:evaluation}

We construct a degree-three correction that reduces the residual with
one row sign and one column sign, and bound the resulting variational
functional.

Exchange rows and columns if necessary, and fix
$\lambda\in(0,1]$ and $p\in\T^2$ with
$a\coloneqq\abs{p_1}\geq\abs{p_2}$.
Put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degree-one-parameters}
  \delta\coloneqq\sqrt\lambda+a,\qquad
  s\coloneqq\sin p_1,\qquad H_0=\lambda+\theta(p)\, .
\end{equation}
We use the shifted variables of \eqref{eq:shift}; sums are taken modulo
$2\pi$, and absolute values use representatives in $(-\pi,\pi]$.
Let $\varphi\in L^2(\T,m)$ be real and even, and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:f}
  f(r)=-i\varphi(r),\qquad w(r)\coloneqq\sin(r)\varphi(r)\, .
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:D1} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degree-one-images}
  D_0^*f=-s\int_\T\varphi\dd m,\qquad (D_1f)_{12}=w\, .
\end{equation}
We seek a correction that reduces the real odd function $w$ and leaves
the two-row residual unchanged.  The correction will add a logarithmic
term to the denominator $\lambda+d(r)+d(\beta)$, giving
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-weight}
\begin{split}
  W(r,\beta)\coloneqq{}&\lambda+d(r)+d(\beta)\\
  &+\frac{\sin^2\beta}{4\pi}
    \log\left(1+\frac\pi{\delta+\abs r+\abs\beta}\right)\, .
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The following lemma bounds the cost of this correction.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:parity}
For every $\lambda\in(0,1]$, $p\in\T^2$ with
$\abs{p_2}\leq\abs{p_1}$, and real even
$\varphi\in L^2(\T,m)$, there is $k\in\Hs_3$ with two row signs and one
column sign in each term such that
$(D_2^*k)_{11}=0$ and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:correction-cost}
\begin{split}
  \Hplus k^2+\Hminus{D_3k}^2
  +\Hminus{(D_1f)_{12}-(D_2^*k)_{12}}^2
  \leq\int_{\T^2}\frac{w(r)^2}{W(r,\beta)}
  \dd m(r)\dd m(\beta)\, .
\end{split}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
To preserve parity in the minimization, we first bound the correction's
energy and the squared norm of its degree-four residual by a weight even
in each frequency.
Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:M}
  M(r,r',\beta)\coloneqq
  4\bigl(\delta+\abs r+\abs{r'}+\abs\beta\bigr)\, .
\end{equation}
For every coefficient function $K\in L^2(\T^3)$ for two rows and one column,
symmetric in $r,r'$ and with the normalization above, we claim
\begin{equation}\label{eq:majorant}
  \Hplus{\Pi_3K}^2+\Hminus{D_3\Pi_3K}^2
  \leq\int_{\T^3}M\abs K^2
  \dd m(r)\dd m(r')\dd m(\beta)\, .
\end{equation}
To integrate the frequency of a newly added sign, use
\begin{equation}\label{eq:line-integral}
  \int_\T\frac{\dd m(t)}{\mu+d(t)}
  =\frac1{\sqrt{\mu(\mu+2)}}\leq\frac1{\sqrt{2\mu}}
  \qquad(\mu>0)\, .
\end{equation}
The substitution $u=\tan(t/2)$ gives the equality.
By \eqref{eq:iso}, the normalization factors for subscripts $1112$, $1122$,
and $112$ are $\sqrt{96}$, $12$, and $\sqrt{18}$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:raise} therefore gives $i\sin\beta/\sqrt3$ times
three summands when a row is added, and $i\sin\alpha/\sqrt2$ times
two summands when a column is added.
Drop the degree-four projection $\Pi_4$ using \eqref{eq:contract}, bound each squared
sum by its number of terms times the sum of squares, and apply
\eqref{eq:line-integral}.  The result is
\begin{align*}
  \Hplus{\Pi_3K}^2+\Hminus{D_3\Pi_3K}^2
  \leq\int_{\T^3}\Biggl[&\lambda+d(\beta)+d(\alpha)
  +\frac{3\sin^2\beta}
  {\sqrt{(\lambda+d(\beta))(\lambda+d(\beta)+2)}}\\
  &+\frac{2\sin^2\alpha}
  {\sqrt{(\lambda+d(\alpha))(\lambda+d(\alpha)+2)}}\Biggr]
  \abs{\Pi_3K}^2\dd m(r)\dd m(r')\dd m(\beta)\, .
\end{align*}
The bracket is a positive continuous function of the total frequency
$(\beta,\alpha)$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:raise} and \eqref{eq:contract}
allow $\Pi_3K$ to be replaced by $K$ in this integral.
To replace the remaining bracket by an even weight, use
$d(t)\leq\abs t$ and
\[
  \frac{\sin^2t}{\sqrt{2d(t)}}
  =2\abs{\sin(t/2)}\cos^2(t/2)\leq\abs t
  \qquad(t\ne0)\, .
\]
Together with $\lambda\leq\sqrt\lambda$ and
$\abs\alpha\leq\abs r+\abs{r'}+a$, these inequalities bound the bracket by
\[
  \lambda+4\abs\beta+3\abs\alpha
  \leq3\delta+3\abs r+3\abs{r'}+4\abs\beta
  \leq M(r,r',\beta)\, .
\]
This proves \eqref{eq:majorant}.  We removed the projection before
comparing with $M$, so the proof does not require $\Pi_3$ to commute with $M$.

We now choose the correction.  Write $W=B+\sigma$, where
\begin{align}
  B(r,\beta)&\coloneqq\lambda+d(r)+d(\beta),\label{eq:B}\\
  \sigma(r,\beta)&\coloneqq W(r,\beta)-B(r,\beta)
  =\sin^2\beta\int_\T\frac{\dd m(r')}{M(r,r',\beta)}\, .\label{eq:sigma}
\end{align}
Integrating $1/(\delta+\abs r+\abs\beta+\abs{r'})$ over $r'$ gives
the logarithm in \eqref{eq:mixed-weight}.
For every real square-integrable $t(r,\beta)$ odd in $r$ and even in
$\beta$, the construction below gives a symmetric coefficient $K$ with
$(\widetilde D_2^*K)_{12}=\sigma t$ and
$\int_{\T^3}M\abs K^2\dd m=\int_{\T^2}\sigma t^2\dd m$.
Thus the minimization reduces to completing the square at each
$(r,\beta)$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalar-energy}
  \sigma t^2+\frac{(w-\sigma t)^2}{B}
  =\frac{w^2}{W}+\frac{\sigma W}{B}
    \left(t-\frac wW\right)^2
  \qquad(t\in\R)\, .
\end{equation}
The minimizing value is $t=w/W$, uniquely when $\sin\beta\ne0$.
At $\sin\beta=0$ the coefficient in \eqref{eq:K} vanishes.

With $t=w/W$, define the symmetric coefficient
\begin{equation}\label{eq:K}
  K(r,r',\beta)\coloneqq
  \frac{i\sin\beta}{\sqrt2 M(r,r',\beta)}
  \left[\frac{w(r)}{W(r,\beta)}+
        \frac{w(r')}{W(r',\beta)}\right]\, .
\end{equation}
The normalization $1/\sqrt2$ accounts for the factor $\sqrt2$ in
\eqref{eq:D2b} and the symmetry of the two square terms in the weighted norm.
We verify the norm and the lowering formulas in four steps.

First, $w/W$ is odd in the row frequency and even in $\beta$, while
$M$ is even in each variable.  Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-cancellation}
  \int_\T\frac{w(r')}{M(r,r',\beta)W(r',\beta)}\dd m(r')=0,
  \qquad (\widetilde D_2^*K)_{12}=\frac{\sigma w}{W}\, .
\end{equation}

Second, when we expand $M\abs K^2$, the cross term integrates to zero by
\eqref{eq:mixed-cancellation}.  The two square terms have equal integrals
by symmetry, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:parity-energy}
  \int_{\T^3}M\abs K^2\dd m(r)\dd m(r')\dd m(\beta)
  =\int_{\T^2}\frac{\sigma w^2}{W^2}\dd m(r)\dd m(\beta)\, .
\end{equation}
The same calculations apply with any real square-integrable function
odd in the row frequency and even in $\beta$ in place of $w/W$.
These identities give the quadratic expression in \eqref{eq:scalar-energy}.

Third, set $k=\Pi_3K$ to remove coefficients with coincident row indices.
The removed part has Fourier transform
\[
  ((I-\Pi_3)K)(r,r',\beta)
  =\int_\T K(t,r+r'-t,\beta)\dd m(t)\, .
\]
Its integral in $r'$ equals the integral of $K$ over both row variables.
Each summand of \eqref{eq:K} has zero such integral by oddness in its
own row variable.  Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:projected-lowering}
  \int_\T((I-\Pi_3)K)(r,r',\beta)\dd m(r')=0,
  \qquad (D_2^*k)_{12}=\frac{\sigma w}{W}\, .
\end{equation}
Fourth, $K$ is odd in $\beta$, and $\Pi_3$ acts only on row indices.
Equation~\eqref{eq:D2a} gives $(D_2^*k)_{11}=0$.

Among symmetric coefficient functions satisfying
$(\widetilde D_2^*K)_{12}=\sigma w/W$, formula~\eqref{eq:K}
uniquely minimizes the weighted norm.
Indeed, if another such function is $K+R$, then \eqref{eq:D2b} gives
\[
  \int_\T R(r,r',\beta)\dd m(r')=0
  \quad\text{for almost every }(r,\beta)\, .
\]
Symmetry and \eqref{eq:K} give $\ip{MK}R=0$ in $L^2(\T^3)$, so
\[
  \norm{\sqrt M(K+R)}_2^2
  =\norm{\sqrt M K}_2^2+\norm{\sqrt M R}_2^2\, .
\]

For fixed $\lambda>0$ and $p$, the bounds $W\geq\lambda$ and
$M\geq4\delta$ give
\[
  \norm K_2\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt2\delta\lambda}\norm\varphi_2\, .
\]
Since $\Pi_3$ contracts the $L^2$ norm, $k\in\Hs_3$ and
$f+k\in L^2(\P)$.  All operators in \eqref{eq:var} are therefore defined
on this test function.
Finally, $w-\sigma w/W=Bw/W$.
Equations~\eqref{eq:majorant}, \eqref{eq:parity-energy}, and
\eqref{eq:projected-lowering} therefore bound the left side of
\eqref{eq:correction-cost} by
\[
  \int_{\T^2}\frac{(\sigma+B)w^2}{W^2}\dd m(r)\dd m(\beta)
  =\int_{\T^2}\frac{w^2}{W}\dd m(r)\dd m(\beta)\, .
\]
\end{proof}

\section{Estimating the quadratic functional}\label{sec:effective-energy}

We integrate out the column frequency to reduce the variational bound
to a quadratic functional of $\varphi$ alone.

Restrict the row frequencies to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Gamma-q}
  \Gamma_\delta\coloneqq
  \{r\in\T:\sqrt\delta\leq\abs r\leq1/4\}
  \qquad(0<\delta<1/256)\, .
\end{equation}
The cutoff gives $\delta\leq r^2$, so the degree-one contribution can
be bounded using the weight
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q}
  q(r)\coloneqq\frac{\abs r}{\sqrt{\log(1/\abs r)}}
  \qquad(0<\abs r\leq1/4)\, .
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:effective-energy}
There is a universal $C<\infty$ such that, for every
$\lambda\in(0,1]$, $p\in\T^2$ with
$\abs{p_2}\leq\abs{p_1}$ and
$\delta=\sqrt\lambda+\abs{p_1}<1/256$, and every real even
$\varphi\in L^2(\T,m)$ vanishing off $\Gamma_\delta$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:effective-energy}
  r_\lambda(p)\leq
  \frac{\left(1-s\int_{\Gamma_\delta}\varphi\dd m\right)^2}{H_0}
  +C\int_{\Gamma_\delta}q(r)\varphi(r)^2\dd m(r)\, .
\end{equation}
The proof gives $C=16$.
\end{lemma}

For each fixed $\delta$, the weight $q$ is bounded above and away from
zero on $\Gamma_\delta$.  Thus the lemma covers every real even measurable
$\varphi$ supported there with finite weighted square integral.

\begin{proof}
Use the coefficients $f,k$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:parity}.
By \eqref{eq:DA}, \eqref{eq:E}, and \eqref{eq:degree-one-images},
orthogonality of the degrees gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:uncancelled}
  \Hplus{f+k}^2+\Hminus{1-A_p(f+k)}^2
  =\frac{\left(1-s\int_\T\varphi\dd m\right)^2}{H_0}+E_p(f,k)\, .
\end{equation}
Set $\mu=\lambda+d(p_1)$.
The coefficient $is/\sqrt2$ in \eqref{eq:D1}, the inequality
$\abs{f(r)+f(r')}^2\leq2\abs{f(r)}^2+2\abs{f(r')}^2$, and
\eqref{eq:contract} give
\[
  \Hminus{(D_1f)_{11}}^2
  \leq\frac{s^2}{2}\int_{\T^2}
  \frac{2\varphi(r)^2+2\varphi(r')^2}
       {\mu+d(r+r'-p_2)}\dd m(r)\dd m(r')
  \leq2s^2\int_\T\varphi(r)^2\dd m(r)
  \int_\T\frac{\dd m(\alpha)}{\mu+d(\alpha)}\, .
\]
The last inequality uses symmetry and the change of variable
$\alpha=r+r'-p_2$.  Thus \eqref{eq:Hsym} and \eqref{eq:line-integral} yield
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degree-one-cost}
\begin{split}
  \Hplus f^2+\Hminus{(D_1f)_{11}}^2
  &\leq\int_\T\left[\mu+d(r)+
    \frac{2s^2}{\sqrt{\mu(\mu+2)}}\right]\varphi(r)^2\dd m(r)\\
  &\leq\int_\T\left(\delta^2+2\sqrt2\delta+\frac{r^2}{2}\right)
    \varphi(r)^2\dd m(r)\, .
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The last inequality uses $\mu\leq\delta^2$ and $s^2\leq2\mu$.
On $\Gamma_\delta$, we have $\delta^2\leq\delta\leq r^2$ and
$\abs r\sqrt{\log(1/\abs r)}\leq1$.
Consequently the last integrand is at most $5q(r)\varphi(r)^2$.
Lemma~\ref{lem:parity} now gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy-reduction}
  E_p(f,k)\leq5\int_{\Gamma_\delta}q(r)\varphi(r)^2\dd m(r)
  +\int_{\T^2}\frac{\sin^2r\varphi(r)^2}{W(r,\beta)}
  \dd m(r)\dd m(\beta)\, .
\end{equation}
It remains to bound the integral of $W^{-1}$ in $\beta$.
Fix $r\in\Gamma_\delta$, put $\rho=\abs r$, and split this integral at
$\abs\beta=\sqrt\rho$.
On the inner interval, $\delta\leq\rho^2$ gives
$\delta+\rho+\abs\beta\leq3\sqrt\rho$ and
$\abs\beta\leq1/2$.  Therefore
\[
  \log\left(1+\frac\pi{\delta+\rho+\abs\beta}\right)
  \geq\frac12\log(1/\rho),\qquad
  W(r,\beta)\geq\frac{2\rho^2}{\pi^2}
    +\frac{\beta^2\log(1/\rho)}{2\pi^3}\, .
\]
The second inequality follows from \eqref{eq:mixed-weight},
\eqref{eq:sine}, and $\abs{\sin\beta}\geq2\abs\beta/\pi$
for $\abs\beta\leq\pi/2$.
On the outer interval, $W\geq d(\beta)\geq2\beta^2/\pi^2$.
Hence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:beta}
\begin{split}
  \int_\T\frac{\dd m(\beta)}{W(r,\beta)}
  &\leq\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R
    \frac{\dd\beta}{2\rho^2/\pi^2+
      \beta^2\log(1/\rho)/(2\pi^3)}
    +\frac\pi2\int_{\sqrt\rho}^\pi\frac{\dd\beta}{\beta^2}\\
  &\leq\frac{\pi^{5/2}}{2\rho\sqrt{\log(1/\rho)}}
    +\frac\pi{2\sqrt\rho}
  \leq\frac{11}{\rho\sqrt{\log(1/\rho)}}\, .
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The last inequality uses $\rho\log(1/\rho)\leq1$ and
$(\pi^{5/2}+\pi)/2<11$.
Since $\sin^2r\leq r^2$, equations~\eqref{eq:energy-reduction} and
\eqref{eq:beta} give $E_p(f,k)\leq16\int_{\Gamma_\delta}q\varphi^2\dd m$.
Substitution in \eqref{eq:uncancelled} and Lemma~\ref{lem:variational-bound}
prove the result with $C=16$.
\end{proof}

\section{The frequency bound}\label{sec:fixed-frequency}

We optimize the degree-one coefficient to obtain a bound that is
integrable over the spatial frequencies, proving transience.

The constant residual in \eqref{eq:effective-energy} depends only on
$\int_{\Gamma_\delta}\varphi\dd m$.
For a fixed value of this integral, Cauchy--Schwarz minimizes
$\int_{\Gamma_\delta}q\varphi^2\dd m$ when $\varphi$ is proportional to
$q^{-1}$ on $\Gamma_\delta$.  This leaves one scalar to optimize.  Set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Z}
\begin{split}
  Z_\delta\coloneqq\int_{\Gamma_\delta}\frac{\dd m(r)}{q(r)}
  &=\frac{2}{3\pi}\left[
    \left(\tfrac12\log(1/\delta)\right)^{3/2}
     -(\log4)^{3/2}\right]\\
  &\asymp[\log(1/\delta)]^{3/2}
  \qquad(0<\delta<1/256)\, .
\end{split}
\end{equation}
The equality follows by substituting $u=\log(1/r)$.
Choose $\varphi(r)=t/q(r)$ on $\Gamma_\delta$ and zero elsewhere,
where $t\in\R$.  This function belongs to $L^2(\T,m)$ because
$\Gamma_\delta$ stays away from zero.  Then
$\int\varphi\dd m=tZ_\delta$ and
$\int q\varphi^2\dd m=t^2Z_\delta$.
Using $C=16$ in \eqref{eq:effective-energy} and completing the square gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalar-minimum}
  \inf_{t\in\R}\left\{\frac{(1-stZ_\delta)^2}{H_0}
    +16t^2Z_\delta\right\}
  =\frac1{H_0+s^2Z_\delta/16},\qquad
  t=\frac{s}{16H_0+s^2Z_\delta}\, .
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optimized}
  r_\lambda(p)\leq\frac1{H_0+s^2Z_\delta/16}\, .
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:frequency}
There is a universal $C<\infty$ such that, for every
$\lambda\in(0,1]$ and $p\in\T^2$, writing
$a(p)=\max\{\abs{p_1},\abs{p_2}\}$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{eq:frequency-bound}
  r_\lambda(p)\leq
  \frac{C}{\lambda+a(p)^2
  [1+\log_+(1/(\sqrt\lambda+a(p)))]^{3/2}}\, .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We verify that $C=2048$ suffices.  Put $\delta=\sqrt\lambda+a(p)$.
If $\delta<1/256$, exchange rows and columns when needed.
Equation~\eqref{eq:Z} gives
\[
  Z_\delta\geq\frac{[\log(1/\delta)]^{3/2}}{6\sqrt2\pi}
  \geq\frac1{32}[\log(1/\delta)]^{3/2}
  \qquad(\delta<1/256)\, .
\]
Indeed, the subtracted term in \eqref{eq:Z} is at most half the first.
Since $s^2\geq4a(p)^2/\pi^2$, the denominator in
\eqref{eq:optimized} is at least
\[
  \lambda+\frac{a(p)^2[\log(1/\delta)]^{3/2}}{128\pi^2}\, .
\]
Since $\log(1/\delta)>5$ and $(6/5)^{3/2}<3/2$, replacing the
logarithm by $1+\log(1/\delta)$ increases the required constant by a factor
less than $3/2$.
Thus $C=2048$ suffices in \eqref{eq:frequency-bound} when $\delta<1/256$.
When $\delta\geq1/256$, we have
$[1+\log_+(1/\delta)]^{3/2}<19$ and $\theta(p)\geq a(p)^2/5$.
The bound \eqref{eq:zero-function} therefore gives
\eqref{eq:frequency-bound} with $C=2048$ in this case as well.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:annealed}]
The square $\{a(p)\leq b\}$ has normalized measure $b^2/\pi^2$ for
$0\leq b\leq\pi$.  On $a(p)\leq\sqrt\lambda$, the bound
$r_\lambda\leq1/\lambda$ therefore contributes at most $1/\pi^2$.
On $\sqrt\lambda<a(p)<1/4$,
$1+\log_+(1/(\sqrt\lambda+a(p)))\geq[1+\log(1/a(p))]/2$.
On $a(p)\geq1/4$, use $r_\lambda(p)\leq\pi^2/(2a(p)^2)$.
Integrating these three bounds, with $C=2048$ from the preceding proof,
gives
\begin{align*}
  \int_{\T^2}r_\lambda(p)\dd m(p)
  &\leq\frac1{\pi^2}
  +\frac{2^{5/2}\cdot2048}{\pi^2}
    \int_0^{1/4}\frac{\dd a}{a[1+\log(1/a)]^{3/2}}+\log(4\pi)\\
  &=\frac1{\pi^2}
  +\frac{2^{7/2}\cdot2048}{\pi^2\sqrt{1+\log4}}+\log(4\pi)
  <2048\, .
\end{align*}
The substitution $u=1+\log(1/a)$ gives the equality.  For the last
inequality, use $\pi^2>9$, $\log4>1$, and $\log(4\pi)<3$ to bound the
expression by $4+8\cdot2048/9<2048$.
If $\sqrt\lambda>1/4$, the first and third regions overlap, which only
enlarges the bound.  Equation~\eqref{eq:green} and monotone convergence
as $\lambda\downarrow0$ prove the explicit Green bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:green-explicit}
  \int_0^\infty\overline p_t(0,0)\dd t\leq2048\, .
\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Tonelli's theorem gives
$\int_0^\infty p_t^\omega(0,0)\dd t<\infty$ almost surely.
The continuous-time walk is the discrete-time walk run with an independent
rate-two Poisson clock, so another application of Tonelli gives
\[
  \int_0^\infty p_t^\omega(0,0)\dd t
  =\sum_{n\geq0}p_n^\omega(0,0)
    \int_0^\infty e^{-2t}\frac{(2t)^n}{n!}\dd t
  =\frac12\sum_{n\geq0}p_n^\omega(0,0)\, .
\]
Finally, $p_n^\omega(x,x)=p_n^{\tau_x\omega}(0,0)$ and $\P$ is
translation invariant.  Intersecting the probability-one events over
$x\in\Z^2$ proves the theorem.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Return probabilities in time}\label{ssec:time}

The symmetric part of the generator bounds the transition probabilities
for every orientation, and the Poisson clock bounds their time derivatives.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:time}
For every deterministic Manhattan orientation $\omega$, all $x,y\in\Z^2$,
and $t\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:heat-kernel}
  p_t^\omega(x,y)\leq\frac2{t+2}\, .
\end{equation}
For $f(t)\coloneqq\overline p_t(0,0)$ and every $t>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:time-derivative}
  \abs{f'(t)}\leq\frac2{\sqrt t(1+t/4)}\leq8t^{-3/2}\, .
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The deterministic heat-kernel estimate follows from
\citet*[Proposition~3, (82), Lemma~5, and Remarks~\textup{(1), (3)}
after its proof]{KT17}.  We give the Nash argument to fix its constant.
Fix $\omega$, and write $P_t=e^{t\mathcal G_\omega}$ and $S_\omega$ for
the symmetric part of $\mathcal G_\omega$ on $\ell^2(\Z^2)$.
By \eqref{eq:discrete-drift}, $S_\omega$ is the rate-two simple-random-walk
generator.  For the Nash inequality, take $0\ne h\in\ell^1(\Z^2)$ and put
$u=\norm h_2^2$, $v=\norm h_1$.
Splitting the Fourier integral at $a(p)=R$, with $0<R\leq\pi$, gives
\[
  u\leq\frac{v^2R^2}{\pi^2}
    +\frac{\pi^2}{2R^2}\ip h{-S_\omega h}\, .
\]
Inside the square, $\abs{\widehat h}\leq v$; outside it,
$\theta(p)\geq2R^2/\pi^2$.  Choosing
$R^2=\pi^2u/(2v^2)\leq\pi^2/2$ therefore gives
\[
  \ip h{-S_\omega h}\geq\frac{\norm h_2^4}{2\norm h_1^2}\, .
\]
Counting measure is invariant, so $P_t\delta_y$ and $P_t^*\delta_x$ are
nonnegative with $\ell^1$ norm one.  The squared norm of $P_t\delta_y$
starts at one and satisfies
\[
  \frac{\dd}{\dd t}\norm{P_t\delta_y}_2^2
  =-2\ip{P_t\delta_y}{-S_\omega P_t\delta_y}
  \leq-\norm{P_t\delta_y}_2^4\, .
\]
The adjoint generator $\mathcal G_\omega^*$ has the same symmetric part.
Applying the same argument to $P_t^*\delta_x$ bounds both squared norms
by $(1+t)^{-1}$.  The semigroup identity and Cauchy--Schwarz give
\[
  p_t^\omega(x,y)
  =\ip{P_{t/2}^*\delta_x}{P_{t/2}\delta_y}
  \leq\frac1{1+t/2}\, .
\]

For the time derivative, let $Q$ be the jump-chain transition matrix.
The independent rate-two Poisson clock gives, for $s>0$,
\[
  P_s=\sum_{n\geq0}e^{-2s}\frac{(2s)^n}{n!}Q^n,\qquad
  \sup_x\sum_y\abs{(\mathcal G_\omega P_s)(x,y)}
  \leq\E\abs{N/s-2}\leq\sqrt{2/s}\, ,
\]
where $N$ has the Poisson distribution of mean $2s$.
The last inequality uses its variance $2s$ and Cauchy--Schwarz;
each $Q^n$ has row sum one.  Factoring
$\mathcal G_\omega P_t=(\mathcal G_\omega P_{t/2})P_{t/2}$ and applying
\eqref{eq:heat-kernel} to the second factor gives
\[
  \abs{\partial_t p_t^\omega(x,y)}
  \leq\frac2{\sqrt t(1+t/4)}\, .
\]
The generator is bounded, so we may differentiate under the environment
average to obtain \eqref{eq:time-derivative}.
\end{proof}

\section{Open questions}\label{sec:open}
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} does not give quantitative estimates.
The heuristic correspondence \eqref{eq:gamma-dimension} gives $\gamma=1/2$,
for which the renormalization-group prediction in
\citet*[(1.3)]{ABK26} is annealed mean-square displacement of order $t^{4/3}$.


\begin{problem}[Mean-square displacement]\label{prob:powerlaw}
Is the annealed mean-square displacement of order $t^{4/3}$ as
$t\to\infty$?
Can the renormalization scheme of \citet*[Sections~1.3 and~3]{ABK26} be
adapted to this setting?
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}[A decay rate]\label{prob:pointwise}
Can one prove that $\overline p_t(0,0)=O(t^{-\beta})$ as $t\to\infty$
for some $\beta>1$?
Redner's displacement-profile prediction \citep*[(3) and p.~290]{Red89}
suggests return probabilities of order $t^{-4/3}$, under the
two-dimensional local-limit hypothesis described in
Section~\ref{sssec:oriented}.
\end{problem}



\begin{problem}[Which orientations are transient]\label{prob:which}
Which orientation fields give a recurrent walk, and which give a transient
one?  Theorem~\ref{thm:main} gives transience for $\P$-almost every
environment.  A uniform random shift by a vector in $\{0,1\}^2$ of the
alternating environment in Section~\ref{ssec:main-results} gives a stationary
ergodic law with mean-zero line orientations under which the walk is
recurrent almost surely.
\end{problem}
